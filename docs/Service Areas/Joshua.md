## City Overview

- **County**: Johnson County, Texas
- **Location**: North-central Johnson County, at the intersection of State Highway 174 and Farm Road 917, on the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad line
- **Population**: Approximately 9,661 (2026 estimate); 7,937 at the 2020 census — a 21.7% increase in six years
- **Annual Growth Rate**: ~3.3% per year, reflecting active suburban expansion
- **Incorporation**: Mid-1950s (Ted Strube served as first mayor)
- **General Character**: Small-town Texas community with deep agricultural and railroad roots that is rapidly transitioning into a suburban growth corridor due to proximity to Fort Worth and Burleson. Residents strongly identify with the local school district, faith community, and the city's rural character even as new subdivisions expand outward.
- **Governance**: Home Rule municipality

### Neighborhoods and Subdivisions

Residential development is densest along the FM 917 corridor and near Joshua High School. Notable neighborhoods include:

- **Heritage Hills** — Well-established neighborhood close to downtown; older homes requiring roof maintenance and replacement
- **Heritage II** — Central corridor; homes mostly 15-20 years old on quarter-acre to half-acre lots
- **Oak Knoll** — Central corridor; similar age range to Heritage II; three- to four-bedroom floor plans
- **Mockingbird Hills** — Central corridor; mix of ages and styles
- **Bluebird Meadows** — Newer, family-friendly subdivision near schools
- **Cooper Valley** — Larger homes and lots; room-to-grow rural character
- **Timber Ridge Estates** — Upscale rural homes with acreage lots; country charm
- **Egan** — Rural fringe neighborhood with a mix of single-family homes and manufactured housing on larger lots
- New construction communities are actively expanding with 261 reported new home communities in the Joshua area across 75 builders, ranging from starter homes to $1.5M rural estates

## History

### Founding and Railroad Origins
- The area's first community was **Caddo Grove**, located two miles west of present-day Joshua; it had a post office and was a thriving agricultural settlement
- In 1881, the Gulf, Colorado and Santa Fe Railway laid tracks from Cleburne northward to Fort Worth — the railroad bypassed Caddo Grove and established a station at the site of future Joshua
- The station was initially called **Caddo Peak**, but the name was rejected by the postal service due to a naming conflict; the name **Joshua** was chosen, reportedly by Dr. D. B. McMillan, after the biblical figure
- The community plat was first surveyed in **1880**, and the town was formally organized in **1881**

### Early Growth Milestones
- **1882** — First store opened by W. L. West; also housed the post office
- **1890** — Population reached 300; town had two steam corn mill-cotton gins, a hotel, a general store, and the _Johnson County Record_ newspaper
- **1890** — First one-room school opened
- **1899** — School relocated to a new building
- **1900 and 1912** — Joshua suffered major fires that impacted early development
- **1904** — Citizens Banking Company opened under J. W. Spencer
- **1906** — Public water system established
- **1914** — Population reached 824; two cotton gins, an ice plant, a bank, the _Joshua Star_ newspaper, and four churches
- **1917** — Joshua High School formally established
- **Mid-1950s** — City incorporated with Ted Strube as first mayor
- **1990** — Population of 3,828, driven by Fort Worth suburban expansion
- **2000** — Population grew to 4,528
- **2020** — Population of 7,937 (census)
- **2026** — Estimated population of ~9,661 with active new home construction underway

### Economic and Cultural Evolution
- The railroad economy centered on shipping agricultural produce — cotton and corn were the dominant crops
- The decline of the Chisholm Trail cattle drives (post-1881) coincided precisely with Joshua's founding, as the railroad replaced the cattle trail economy
- Modern growth is driven almost entirely by Joshua's position as an affordable community within commuting distance of Fort Worth, Burleson, and the broader DFW metroplex

## Weather and Storm Patterns

### Climate Overview
- **Summer**: Hot and humid; average high in August is 95.5°F, with roof surface temperatures routinely reaching 150-170°F
- **Winter**: Cold and windy; average January low is 39°F; temperatures rarely drop below 24°F but freezes do occur
- **Annual Rainfall**: Approximately 35-37 inches per year; 135 rainfall days annually
- **Peak Rainfall Month**: October (approximately 4.21 inches)
- **Snow**: Rare; approximately 2.2 snowfall days per year, ~1.18 inches accumulated on average
- **General Pattern**: Partly cloudy year-round; hot humid summers, mild winters with periodic hard freezes

### Severe Weather and Hail

Johnson County is one of the most storm-active counties in the DFW region. Key data points:

- **903 recorded severe weather events** in Johnson County between 1951 and 2026
- **374 hail events** on record — hail is the single dominant hazard, accounting for **41.4% of all severe weather events**
- **April** is the peak month for hail, with 149 recorded events historically
- Doppler radar has detected hail at or near Joshua on **86 occasions**
- The Joshua area has had 6 confirmed on-the-ground hail reports from trained spotters
- **$253.8 million** in total recorded property damage from severe weather in Johnson County

### Tornado Risk
- **104 mapped tornado paths** intersect Johnson County; 102 touched down inside the county boundary
- Tornadoes represent the costliest hazard by damage ($235.2M in reported losses)
- Joshua sits in the southern reach of Tornado Alley, where warm moist Gulf air collides with cool dry continental air
- **April 3, 2012** — A tornado near Joshua received a preliminary NWS rating of EF-1 with winds up to 100 mph; path length 1.1 miles, maximum width 60 yards
- **March 13, 2019** — A severe storm moved through Johnson County; a 40x60-foot metal barn was deposited on a Joshua home; NWS recorded wind speeds near 75 mph in nearby Godley
- The DFW NWS office in Fort Worth (weather.gov/fwd) actively monitors this corridor

### Severe Weather Season
- **Primary season**: March through June, with April being peak for hail and severe thunderstorms
- **Secondary risk window**: October-November for isolated severe events
- **Winter ice risk**: Hard freezes and ice storms occur most years; freeze-thaw cycles cause shingle cracking, sealant failure, and loosened fasteners; ice dams can form when attic heat melts roof ice and refreezing blocks drainage at eaves

### How North Texas Climate Affects Roofing Materials
- **UV Degradation**: Intense solar radiation in summer accelerates granule loss and asphalt binder breakdown
- **Heat Expansion**: Roof surface temps of 150-170°F cause repeated thermal expansion and contraction, stressing fasteners and seams
- **Reduced Lifespan**: A 30-year architectural shingle in moderate climates may last 25-28 years; in DFW conditions the same product typically delivers 15-20 years of service — a 30-40% reduction
- **Wind Stress**: High straight-line wind events (common in spring) lift shingle edges and break adhesive seals
- **Freeze-Thaw Cycling**: Winter freeze cycles crack aged sealant and expose underlayment to moisture intrusion
- **Hail Impact**: 2-inch hailstones can destroy shingle granule coating in a single storm; Class 4 impact-resistant shingles are strongly recommended throughout this area

## Roofing and Restoration Industry Relevance

### Common Roofing Materials in the Area
- **Architectural (dimensional) asphalt shingles** — By far the most prevalent material in Joshua; standard on the majority of homes built in the 1990s through present; GAF, CertainTeed, and Owens Corning are the leading manufacturers used in the market
- **3-tab asphalt shingles** — Found on older homes built before the mid-1990s; these are at or past end of life and represent the highest replacement opportunity
- **Metal roofing** — Growing in popularity for rural and larger-lot homes (Timber Ridge Estates, Cooper Valley, Egan); excellent for heat resistance and hail durability
- **Synthetic and composite shingles** — Emerging option; marketed as Class 4 impact-resistant alternatives
- **Tile roofing** — Limited; found on higher-end custom homes

### Roof Age and Replacement Cycles
- Homes in Heritage Hills and established in-town neighborhoods were largely built in the 1980s-2000s — many roofs are 20-35 years old and at or past end of useful life
- Heritage II, Oak Knoll, and Mockingbird Hills (built 2000s-2010s) have roofs that are 15-25 years old — entering the inspection and potential replacement window
- New construction subdivisions (Bluebird Meadows, Cooper Valley expansion, etc.) represent future maintenance and storm-damage restoration customers
- Rapid population growth (21.7% since 2020) means a growing base of new homeowners unfamiliar with local storm risk — a prime audience for roof inspection campaigns

### Rural vs. Subdivision Roofing Considerations
- **Subdivision homes**: Tighter lot spacing means one hail storm typically damages an entire block; strong word-of-mouth and neighbor referral dynamics
- **Rural properties (Egan, Timber Ridge, acreage lots)**: Often have metal buildings, barns, and detached structures in addition to the main residence — multiple roofing scopes per property; longer response time from national contractors creates local opportunity
- **Manufactured and mobile homes**: Present in rural pockets of the Joshua area; require specialty roofing approaches

### Insurance Claim Patterns
- Johnson County's 374 hail events create a consistent cadence of insurance claims; roofing companies operating here should maintain strong relationships with local independent insurance agents
- Texas law gives homeowners two years from the date of a storm to file a hail damage claim; timely post-storm door-to-door canvassing and inspection offers are effective
- Class 4 impact-resistant shingles qualify homeowners for insurance premium discounts — a compelling sales angle that goes beyond basic replacement

### Local Building Codes and Permit Requirements
- Joshua requires **all roofing contractors to register with the city** before beginning work; registration requires proof of liability insurance
- **Permits are required** for most roofing projects; work is subject to a final inspection confirming proper underlayment, fastener spacing, flashing, ventilation, and shingle layer count
- **Permit fee**: $100.00 for roofing decking; residential repairs and alterations are charged at $0.35 per square foot with a $100 minimum
- Joshua follows the **2021 International Residential Code (IRC)** as the Texas state baseline, with potential local amendments
- Contact the City of Joshua Building Department directly for current requirements before pulling permits
