## City Overview

### Key Statistics
- **Population:** ~403,700 (2024 estimate) — 7th largest city in Texas, 50th largest in the U.S.
- **County:** Tarrant County (entirely)
- **Area:** 95.9 square miles
- **Population Density:** ~3,811 people per square mile
- **Median Household Income:** ~$60,571
- **Homeownership Rate:** ~55% owner-occupied, 45% renter-occupied
- **Incorporation:** Arlington was formally platted in 1876 with the arrival of the Texas and Pacific Railway

### General Character
- Centrally located between Dallas and Fort Worth — often called the "Midpoint City" of DFW
- Nationally known as an entertainment and sports destination (Cowboys, Rangers, Six Flags, Hurricane Harbor)
- Home to the University of Texas at Arlington (UTA), a major anchor institution
- Diverse, working-class to middle-class city with a mix of longtime residents and newer growth
- Significant suburban sprawl — a car-dependent city without a major downtown transit hub
- Languages: 33.5% of residents speak a non-English language at home (22.4% Spanish)

### Neighborhoods and Subdivisions

**North Arlington (newer, higher-income growth corridor)**
- **Viridian** — Master-planned community on 2,000 acres along the West Fork of the Trinity River; 5 lakes, 1,000 acres of open space; mix of traditional and contemporary homes; strict HOA with CC&Rs
- **Elements at Viridian** — 55+ active adult section within Viridian
- **Interlochen** — Established neighborhood near Collins Street corridor
- **North Arlington / River Legacy area** — Family-oriented neighborhoods near River Legacy Park

**Central Arlington (established, 1970s–1990s housing stock)**
- **Pantego** — Incorporated municipality within Arlington's footprint; small-town feel; ranch-style homes built 1960s–1990s; top-rated schools
- **Dalworthington Gardens** — Another small incorporated municipality surrounded by Arlington
- **Park Row / Matlock Road corridor** — Dense residential suburbs, middle-income families
- **Meadowbrook** — Older established neighborhood near UTA
- **Webb-Hester** — Historic neighborhood near downtown Arlington
- **Woodland Hills** — Family neighborhood in central Arlington

**South Arlington (1980s–2000s suburbs, significant inventory)**
- **Sublett Road corridor** — Active residential area near Mansfield border
- **Stone Creek** — Popular subdivision in south Arlington
- **Summer Creek** — South Arlington near Hwy 287
- **Arlington Highlands area** — Near the outdoor shopping center, mix of ages
- **Southeast Arlington (76002, 76018)** — High concentration of 1990s–2000s homes due for first or second roof replacement

**West Arlington (older stock near Six Flags / I-30 corridor)**
- **Lake Arlington area** — Homes surrounding the city's reservoir; older, established
- **Handley-Ederville** — Historic east Fort Worth / west Arlington borderland
- **Shady Valley** — Near Lake Arlington; mix of ages and price points

## History

### Founding & Early Development
- The area was first settled in the 1840s–1850s as part of Johnson Station, a frontier trading post
- **1876:** The Texas and Pacific Railway chose the site for a stop on its transcontinental route; the town was platted and named "Arlington" after Arlington House, the Virginia estate of Confederate General Robert E. Lee
- The railroad spurred rapid growth from an agricultural outpost into a regional trading town
- Major crops: cotton, corn, wheat; livestock trade was also significant
- **1884:** Arlington incorporated as a city
- **1902:** Discovery of mineral wells led to the development of Arlington Mineral Well, a health resort popular across Texas; briefly made Arlington a regional tourism destination

### Growth Through the 20th Century
- **1895:** Carlisle Military Academy established — it would eventually evolve into the University of Texas at Arlington
- **1920s:** Population ~3,000; still a small agricultural town
- **1940s:** WWII industrial boom brought workers and families from across Texas to find manufacturing jobs
- **1950s–1960s:** Post-war suburban expansion began rapidly; I-30 and I-20 corridor development drove population from ~7,700 (1950) to ~90,000 (1970) as Dallas–Fort Worth suburbs exploded
- **1961:** Six Flags Over Texas opened — positioned Arlington as a major entertainment destination and began its transformation from suburb to destination city
- **1965:** Texas A&M Arlington branch joined the University of Texas System as the University of Texas at Arlington
- **1972:** Texas Rangers MLB franchise began play at Arlington Stadium — placed Arlington on the national sports map
- **1994:** The Ballpark in Arlington (now Choctaw Stadium) opened as the Rangers' new home; became an architectural landmark
- **1990s–2000s:** Massive residential and commercial development, particularly in south and west Arlington; population grew from ~261,000 (1990) to ~365,000 (2010)

### Recent Development
- **2009:** Dallas Cowboys relocated from Irving to a new $1.2B domed stadium in Arlington (AT&T Stadium); Arlington approved the funding via public referendum
- **2015:** Arlington voters approved public funding to retain the Texas Rangers and build a new stadium
- **2018:** Texas Live! entertainment complex opened adjacent to the stadiums
- **2020:** Globe Life Field (retractable roof) opened as the new Rangers home; hosted the 2020 World Series (only fans-in-attendance World Series during COVID)
- The entertainment district now anchors the city's identity as a tourism destination, drawing millions of annual visitors

## Weather & Storm Patterns

### Climate Overview
- Arlington sits in the **humid subtropical climate zone** (transitioning to semi-arid)
- Summers are long, hot, and humid; winters are mild to cool with periodic severe cold snaps
- **Average annual rainfall:** ~29.72 inches (distributed across ~136 rain days per year)
- Peak rainfall months: **October (3.98") and May** — note that fall storms can produce significant roof damage
- **Summer highs:** Regularly exceed 100°F; average July high ~97°F
- **Winter lows:** January average low ~39°F; freezes occur several times per year
- **Temperature swings:** 30–40°F swings in a single day are not uncommon, creating significant thermal stress on roofing materials

### Hail Risk — High Priority
- Arlington sits in **"Hail Alley"** — the DFW corridor is among the most hail-active zones in the entire country
- DFW averages **6–8 significant hail events per year**
- Hail sizes range from pea-sized (cosmetic granule loss) to baseball-sized (structural compromise)
- **Peak hail season:** April and May are statistically the most active months; March and June are secondary risk months
- Fall transition storms (September–November) produce an additional secondary risk window
- Roughly **1 in 10 Texas homes files a hail-related insurance claim over any 5-year period**
- Dallas is the **most hail-claimed metro in the United States**

### Notable Historic Storms
- **March 2016:** Egg-to-tennis-ball-sized hail struck Arlington and surrounding Tarrant County; insured losses in Tarrant County exceeded **$600 million**; one of the costliest hail events in the region's history
- **April 3, 2012:** Kennedale-Arlington tornado caused tree and roof damage near AT&T Stadium and Globe Life Park area; confirmed EF-1 touchdown
- **June 2023:** Massive DFW hail outbreak produced **$7–10 billion in insured losses** across the metro
- **May 2024:** Additional significant outbreak produced **$2.3 billion in Texas hail damage** (NOAA)
- **2025:** Tornado touched down in the AT&T Stadium / Globe Life Field corridor; snapped trees and stripped roofing off structures

### Tornado Risk
- Arlington sits within **Tornado Alley**
- The National Weather Service (NWS Fort Worth) regularly issues tornado warnings for Tarrant County between March and June
- While direct tornado hits are less common than hail, wind-shear damage and near-miss events still cause significant roof and structural damage each year
- Straight-line wind events (60+ mph gusts from severe thunderstorm lines) cause shingle loss, fascia damage, and soffit damage throughout the city every year

### Winter Weather Risk
- Ice storms affect North Texas every few years and can cause significant damage:
  - Ice accumulation on roofs adds weight stress
  - Freeze-thaw cycles open up existing cracks and damaged flashing
  - Wind-driven sleet removes vulnerable shingles on aging roofs
- Notable winter events: the **February 2021 Winter Storm Uri** was catastrophic for the region — burst pipes, structural stress, and widespread roof failures across the DFW area
- **Ice dams** are less common than in northern states but can form during extended cold snaps when attic insulation is inadequate

### How the North Texas Climate Damages Roofs
- **UV degradation:** Extreme UV index (peak: 10+ in summer) breaks down asphalt shingle granules and binder faster than in northern climates — shortening standard 30-year shingles to an effective 15–20 year lifespan in Arlington
- **Thermal expansion/contraction:** Daily temperature swings cause roofing materials to expand and contract repeatedly, stressing fasteners, flashing, and ridge caps
- **Heat cycling:** Attic temperatures in summer can exceed 150°F, accelerating shingle aging from the underside
- **Wind uplift:** Severe storm winds can lift shingles that have lost flexibility due to heat aging
- **Hail impact:** The most common driver of insurance-covered roof replacement in the area
- **Moisture intrusion:** Heavy spring rainfall combined with clogged gutters accelerates fascia rot and decking damage

## Roofing & Restoration Industry Relevance

### Common Roofing Materials
- **Architectural (dimensional) asphalt shingles** — The dominant roofing material in Arlington; used in the vast majority of residential replacements; effective lifespan 15–20 years in the DFW climate
- **3-tab asphalt shingles** — Found on older homes (pre-2000); largely being replaced with architectural shingles; less wind-resistant and more hail-vulnerable
- **Impact-Resistant (Class 4) shingles** — Growing in demand due to insurance incentives; many insurers now offer discounts or require them in high-risk zip codes
- **Metal roofing (standing seam and metal shingles)** — Growing market segment; 50+ year lifespan; superior heat reflectivity and hail resistance; appealing to homeowners tired of repeated storm claims
- **Clay/concrete tile** — Less common but found in higher-end subdivisions and some Mediterranean-style homes
- **Flat/low-slope roofing (TPO, EPDM)** — Found on commercial properties and some contemporary residential additions

### Housing Stock & Replacement Cycles
Arlington's housing stock spans six decades of construction, creating a wide range of roof ages:
- **Pre-1970 homes** (near UTA, downtown core): Second or third roof replacement overdue; likely have original fascia, aged decking, and outdated flashing around HVAC penetrations
- **1970s–1980s homes** (central and west Arlington, Lake Arlington area, Pantego): First or second roof replacement cycle; common issues include failing 3-tab shingles, aged gutters, and deteriorated ridge vents
- **1990s–2000s homes** (south Arlington, 76002, 76017, 76018 zip codes): High inventory of homes reaching the 20–25 year mark — prime candidates for full replacement, especially after any storm event
- **2000s–2010s homes** (growing outer suburbs): Mid-life; hail inspection focus after major storm events
- **2010s–present** (Viridian, new north Arlington developments): Newer stock; inspection and minor repair focus; HOA-governed

**Key Insight:** A substantial portion of Arlington's residential inventory was built in the 1985–2005 window. These homes are now 20–40 years old and represent the highest concentration of roof replacement demand in the city.

### HOA Considerations
- **Newer master-planned communities (Viridian, stone-gate subdivisions):** Strict CC&Rs with approved shingle palettes, color restrictions, and required Architectural Committee approval before replacement
- **Post-2000 south Arlington subdivisions:** Typically have HOA oversight with material and color specifications
- **Older central and west Arlington neighborhoods:** Light or no HOA; homeowners have more flexibility in material and color selection
- **Pantego and Dalworthington Gardens:** Each is its own municipality with independent permitting; separate permit process from Arlington proper

### Permits & Building Code
- The **City of Arlington Building Inspection Division** issues re-roofing permits and conducts construction inspections
- A permit is required for roof replacements and for repairs exceeding **10% of the total roof surface**
- Tarrant County and the City of Arlington follow the **International Building Code (IBC)** and **International Residential Code (IRC)** as adopted by Texas
- Contractors must be licensed; homeowners pulling their own permits may face resale complications
- Wind resistance ratings and fastener patterns are inspected on permit jobs
- **Tip:** Permit-pulled jobs are a trust signal — 41 Roofing pulling permits demonstrates professionalism to homeowners

### Insurance Claim Patterns
- Hail storms generate the majority of insurance-covered roof replacements in the Arlington market
- Carriers have aggressively shifted from **1% to 2% wind/hail deductibles** between 2022 and 2024 — homeowners need to understand their policy before filing
- Some insurers have stopped writing new policies in high-risk DFW zip codes unless the home has Class 4 (impact-resistant) roofing installed
- Post-storm, public adjusters and storm-chasing contractors flood the market — professional, locally-rooted contractors who document damage properly and navigate claims ethically stand out
- Supplement billing and Xactimate proficiency are important for maximizing claim settlements for homeowners
- **Common claim triggers:** Hail (primary), straight-line wind, tornado proximity damage, ice storms
