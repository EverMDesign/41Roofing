## City Overview

- **County**: Johnson County — Cleburne is the county seat
- **Population**: ~31,352 (2020 Census); estimated ~38,131 as of 2024, reflecting steady growth
- **Location**: Approximately 25 miles south of Fort Worth, situated between the Grand Prairie and Blackland Prairie regions
- **Incorporation**: May 1871 (settlement established March 23, 1867)
- **Character**: Small-to-mid-size Texas city with a strong railroad heritage, agricultural roots, and a tight-knit community identity; growing bedroom community for Fort Worth/DFW workers who want space and lower costs without sacrificing proximity to the metro
- **Government**: Council-manager form; city operates eight municipal parks and Lake Pat Cleburne

### Neighborhoods and Subdivisions

Cleburne's residential footprint spans a mix of historic inner-city blocks and newer suburban-style subdivisions pushing outward, all representing roofing work opportunities at different stages of the asset lifecycle.

- **Historic Downtown / Inner-City Blocks** — Pre-WWII and mid-century homes along and near the Town Square; older housing stock with wood sheathing, original decks, and aging shingles requiring full replacement rather than repair
- **Westhill** — Established neighborhood in the 76033 zip code with 1960s–1980s ranch-style construction; the Westhill Park neighborhood "square" anchors this area
- **Winchester** — Family-oriented subdivision with mix of 1990s and early 2000s construction; school-age households = high insurance-claim engagement
- **Belclaire** — Newer development with Belclaire Community Park; typical 2000s–2010s architectural shingle construction entering the 15–20 year replacement window
- **Hulen Park / West Buffalo Creek Corridor** — Some of Cleburne's oldest residential stock; homes near the creek and park attract buyers who renovate, creating re-roofing demand
- **Lake Pat Cleburne Surroundings** — Lakeside and rural residential properties on the city's south side; mix of older lake cabins and newer custom builds
- **Surrounding Rural/Unincorporated Johnson County** — Farm-to-market roads south and west of Cleburne hold scattered rural residential, metal agricultural buildings, and newer acreage developments
- **New Construction Corridors** — Active residential growth along US-67 and Nolan River Road corridors as DFW sprawl reaches Johnson County

## Local Landmarks & Points of Interest

### Historic Downtown & Town Square

- **Johnson County Courthouse** — Centerpiece of Cleburne's Town Square; built 1913 (Lang & Witchell architects); Beaux Arts plan with Prairie Style elements, Sullivanesque detailing, pink granite exterior, six-story interior atrium with an art glass dome, and a prominent clock tower; listed on the National Register of Historic Places (April 14, 1988); a Recorded Texas Historic Landmark (1999); rededicated December 1, 2007 after restoration; houses the Johnson County Courthouse Museum
- **Cleburne Town Square (Johnson County Market Square)** — The historic center of civic life; traces its origin to 1854 log cabin settlement; hosts farmers markets, festivals, and the Cleburne Wine & Art Festival
- **Layland Museum of History** — Housed in the 1905 former public library building (Beaux Arts / Classical Revival architecture); archives photographs, documents, and artifacts of local history
- **Plaza Theatre Company / Dudley Hall** — 276-seat theatre-in-the-round in historic downtown; locally beloved cultural anchor
- **Helmcamp Community Theatre** — Second performing arts venue downtown

### Railroad Heritage

- **Cleburne Intermodal Transportation Depot (Historic Santa Fe Depot)** — The physical symbol of Cleburne's railroad identity; anchor of the railroad heritage district
- **Cleburne Railroad Museum** — Documents the city's deep Santa Fe Railway history
- **Chisholm Trail Outdoor Museum** — Outdoor living history museum celebrating the Chisholm Trail; features the oldest surviving log courthouse in Texas (1855 original); hosts the Largest Intertribal Gathering in North Texas

### Parks & Natural Landmarks

- **Cleburne State Park** — Located south of the city along Nolan River; 528 acres with Cedar Lake, hiking trails, camping, fishing, and swimming; one of the original eight Texas State Parks built by the Civilian Conservation Corps (1930s); a strong local identity anchor
- **Hulen Park** — 19.8-acre city park along West Buffalo Creek; one of the oldest parks in Cleburne; home of Whistle Stop Christmas lights display
- **Lake Pat Cleburne** — Municipal reservoir on the city's south side; recreation area with boating and fishing
- **Big Rocks Park** — Natural rock formations with hiking, swimming, and picnicking; a hidden gem locals know
- **Westhill Park, Stone Park, Belclaire Community Park** — Neighborhood parks distributed through residential areas
- **Tolbert and Margaret Mayfield Sports Complex** — Active youth sports hub; strong community traffic

### Schools & Educational Institutions

- **Cleburne Independent School District (CISD)** — Primary school district; multiple campuses anchoring neighborhoods
- **Alvarado ISD, Joshua ISD, Rio Vista ISD** — School districts also serving the broader 76031 zip code area
- **Hill College (Cleburne Extension)** — Community college presence

### Shopping & Commercial Centers

- **Cleburne Town Square area** — Main retail and dining district
- **US-67 commercial corridor** — Chain retail, auto dealers, service businesses
- **Walmart, Home Depot** — Anchor box retail on the north side

### Churches

Cleburne has a dense presence of established churches, consistent with its small-city Texas character — First Baptist, First Methodist, First Presbyterian, and numerous independent congregations. Many have large congregations that represent significant community trust networks.

## History

### Founding

- Johnson County created by the Texas Legislature in **1854**; original county seat was Buchanan
- In **1867**, county leadership relocated the seat to a more central location; the new settlement was named **Cleburne** in honor of **Major General Patrick Ronayne Cleburne**, a Confederate general under whom many local settlers had served during the Civil War
- Patrick Cleburne was an Irish immigrant who became one of the South's most capable battlefield commanders; he died at the Battle of Franklin (November 30, 1864) — making him one of the highest-ranking Confederate officers killed in the war
- The town was incorporated **May 1871**

### Railroad Development — The Defining Era

- **October 21, 1881**: The first railroad (Gulf, Colorado and Santa Fe) arrived in Cleburne, greeted by over 1,000 spectators — a defining moment in the city's growth
- A secondary line connecting Weatherford to Cleburne was completed **1887**
- **1897–1899**: Cleburne beat out Dallas, Fort Worth, and Temple to become the site of the **largest Santa Fe Railway repair shops west of the Mississippi**; the shops repaired and manufactured locomotives and rail cars; they doubled the city's population in the 1890s
- The Santa Fe Shops became the **largest employer in the area for nearly a century**
- The shops closed in **1989**, leaving a generational economic shift the city has steadily recovered from through diversification and DFW bedroom-community growth

### Agricultural Roots

- Johnson County's fertile Blackland Prairie soil made it prime cotton and grain farming country through the late 19th and early 20th centuries
- Agricultural fairs, rodeos, and livestock traditions remain culturally embedded — the Johnson County Sheriff's Posse PRCA Rodeo is still an annual institution
- The Chisholm Trail passed through the area; cattle drives were a formative economic force before the railroad arrived

### Johnson County Courthouse History

- First courthouse built shortly after county formation (1854); earliest structure was a log cabin
- The 1883 Second Empire-style courthouse burned down **April 15, 1912**
- The current **1913 courthouse** (Lang & Witchell) replaced it; it remains one of the finest courthouse buildings in Texas and was restored and rededicated in 2007 with Texas Courthouse Preservation Program support

### Key Milestones

- 1862: Camp Henderson established near Buffalo Creek (Civil War period)
- 1867: Town formally platted as county seat, named Cleburne
- 1871: Incorporated
- 1881: Railroad arrives
- 1897–1899: Santa Fe Shops established — population doubles
- 1913: Current courthouse built
- 1930s: Cleburne State Park built by Civilian Conservation Corps
- 1989: Santa Fe Shops close
- 2013: Major tornado outbreak strikes the city
- 2024: Estimated population exceeds 38,000; continued growth as DFW suburban expansion reaches Johnson County

## Weather & Storm Patterns

### Climate Summary

- **Climate type**: Humid subtropical (Cfa) — large seasonal temperature swings amplified by inland location
- **Annual precipitation**: ~40 inches (1,012 mm); wettest month is May (~5.9 inches)
- **Temperature range**: Typically 37°F (winter lows) to 96°F (summer highs); rarely below 25°F or above 102°F
- **Average annual temperature**: ~66.7°F
- **Thunderstorm days**: Approximately 23 per year on average
- **Humidity**: Summer months (June–August) feel uncomfortably humid; contributes to mold growth under damaged roofing

### Severe Weather — Hail

- Cleburne and Johnson County sit within **"Hail Alley"** — the corridor of Central and North Texas where conditions for large hail are nearly optimal every spring
- Doppler radar has detected hail at or near Cleburne on **120+ occasions** in recent years
- Trained storm spotters have filed **46+ on-the-ground hail reports** from the immediate Cleburne area
- The area has been under severe weather warnings **55+ times in a recent 12-month period**
- Peak hail season: **March through June**, driven by Gulf of Mexico moisture colliding with cold continental air masses
- Hail sizes range from pea-sized to softball-sized (4-inch hail recorded in Johnson County in April 2026)
- Hail is the **#1 cause of loss for home insurance in Texas**, accounting for ~40% of all filed homeowners claims statewide
- Average Texas hail claim: **$11,000–$15,000**, primarily driven by roof damage

### Severe Weather — Tornadoes

- **May 15, 2013** — The most significant recent event: an **EF-3 tornado** struck Cleburne with estimated winds of 136–165 mph and a maximum path width of 0.6 miles; **600 homes were damaged**; 9 people sustained minor injuries; the tornado struck at night, compounding danger; local residents still reference this storm as the benchmark disaster
- The same 2013 storm system produced devastating tornadoes in nearby Granbury (6 fatalities)
- **1997 Central Texas tornado outbreak** (May 27, 1997): 20 confirmed tornadoes, F5 maximum rating, 28 fatalities, $126.6 million in damage across the broader region
- The area lies near the southern edge of **Tornado Alley** and faces recurring tornado watch/warning seasons each spring
- April 2026: Tornado warnings again issued for Cleburne, Keene, and Joshua — residents on Lakeshore Drive specifically braced for impact, citing the 2013 memory

### Severe Weather — Wind

- Straight-line winds from thunderstorm downbursts are common and regularly cause shingle blow-off, fascia damage, and gutter failures
- Derecho-type wind events can impact the area in summer

### Winter Weather

- Ice storms are a recurring risk; the February 2021 "Winter Storm Uri" was catastrophic across all of North Texas
- Cleburne is slightly more protected from Arctic blasts than the Panhandle but still vulnerable to ice accumulation
- Ice loading on roofs, ice damming at eaves, and freeze-thaw cycles on flashing are documented concerns
- Snow is rare but freezing rain and sleet occur most winters

### How North Texas Climate Damages Roofing Materials

- **UV degradation**: Summer sun intensity in North Texas accelerates granule loss on asphalt shingles; a 20-year shingle in this climate often performs more like a 15-year shingle
- **Heat expansion/contraction**: Daily and seasonal thermal cycling causes flashing to loosen, sealant to crack, and ridge cap to separate over time
- **Hail impact**: Granule displacement from hail exposes asphalt mat, dramatically shortening shingle lifespan and voiding manufacturer warranties
- **Wind uplift**: Shingles installed without proper nailing patterns (common in older homes) are especially vulnerable; wind ratings matter more in North Texas than in calmer regions
- **Moisture**: ~40 inches of annual rain with high summer humidity creates conditions for algae streaking, wood deck rot if shingles are compromised, and attic moisture accumulation

## Roofing & Restoration Industry Relevance

### Common Roofing Materials

- **Architectural (dimensional) asphalt shingles** — By far the dominant material in Cleburne; most 1980s–2010s construction used standard 3-tab or dimensional shingles; many of these are now at or past their useful life
- **Metal roofing** — Growing in popularity, particularly on rural properties, agricultural structures, and with homeowners making a long-term investment after storm damage; standing seam and corrugated steel are both common
- **Flat/low-slope modified bitumen or TPO** — Used on commercial buildings and some older flat-roof residential structures in the historic district
- **Tile (concrete and clay)** — Less common than shingles but present on some higher-end homes; requires specialized installation knowledge
- **Wood shake** — Present on some older pre-1990 homes; increasingly replaced with Class A fire-rated alternatives per current code

### Home Age and Roof Replacement Cycles

- **Median construction year in Cleburne: 1983** — This means the largest cohort of the housing stock is approximately 40+ years old; roofing on these homes has likely been replaced once and is approaching (or past) a second replacement cycle
- Homes built before 1940: ~5.5% of stock — historic homes with complex rooflines, original wood decking, and potential code-compliance issues on re-roof
- Homes built 1940–1979: substantial share — standard 3-tab shingle era; decks may be 1x skip sheathing rather than plywood
- Homes built 1980–1999: largest cohort — architectural shingle era; 25–30-year shingles placed at construction are now failing
- Homes built 2000–2009: ~14.4% — these are entering the 15–20 year replacement window, especially if impacted by hail
- Homes built 2010–2019: ~9% — newer stock but still hail-impacted; insurance claim work is relevant here
- New construction (2020+): ~5% — new roofing installation opportunities; builders look for reliable subcontractors

### Historic Homes Considerations

- Downtown historic district homes often require permit coordination with city historic preservation guidelines
- Older homes may have multiple layers of roofing that must be removed before re-roofing (Texas building code limits layers)
- Original wood decks may be rotted or structurally compromised; deck replacement adds scope and revenue
- Chimney flashing, skylights, and dormers are more common on historic homes; specialization pays

### Insurance Claim Patterns

- North Texas insurance carriers have dramatically tightened underwriting due to repetitive hail losses; homeowners frequently face non-renewal or steep premium increases after claims
- This creates urgency to repair documented damage quickly and document thoroughly
- The 2013 tornado generated a major wave of insurance claims in Cleburne; the 2026 storm season is tracking similarly
- Many homeowners are unaware they have hail damage until a roofer performs a free inspection — proactive canvassing after storms is standard industry practice in this market
- Public adjusters are active in Johnson County; roofing companies that can work efficiently with adjusters have a competitive advantage

### Building Codes & Permit Requirements

- The City of Cleburne adopted the **2021 ICC codes and 2020 NEC** with NCTCOG amendments, effective December 1, 2022
- **A roofing permit is required when replacing decking or rafters**; repair-only re-shingle jobs may not always require a permit but verify with the city
- Class A fire-rated roof coverings are required for new construction and major re-roofing projects
- Minimum attic insulation requirement: **R-49** (increased under recent code cycle)
- Contact: Cleburne Community Development Department — **817-645-0955**
- Apply for permits: `cleburne.net/1343/Apply-for-a-Building-Permit`

## Community Connection Points

### Signature Annual Events

- **Whistle Stop Christmas** — Cleburne's most iconic annual event; millions of lights in Hulen Park and downtown, lit nightly from the Monday before Thanksgiving through December 31; includes a Lighted Christmas Parade (first Friday of December), historic home tour, food trucks, and vendors; named for the city's railroad heritage; draws visitors from across Johnson County and DFW
- **Cleburne Wine & Art Festival** — Last Saturday of September; Johnson County Market Square; Texas wines, food trucks, arts/crafts, live music
- **Spring Fest** — Early May; food, music, arts and crafts; family-friendly
- **Autumn on the Square** — Late October; family activities, local business support
- **Johnson County Sheriff's Posse PRCA Rodeo** — Professional rodeo; bareback, saddle bronc, bull riding, barrel racing; deeply rooted in agricultural tradition
- **Shakespeare in the Park** — Presented by Plaza Theatre Company and Cleburne Chamber of Commerce at Winston Patrick McGregor Park
- **Chisholm Trail Intertribal Gathering** — Largest intertribal event in North Texas; held at the Chisholm Trail Outdoor Museum; powwow, native music, educational displays

### Community Organizations

- **Cleburne Chamber of Commerce** — Chartered February 17, 1919; 950+ members; primary business networking organization; annual events listed above; `cleburnechamber.com`
- **Cleburne Tuesday Forum** — Nonprofit association for public discussion of issues affecting Johnson County; well-attended civic forum
- **Johnson County Historical Commission** — Preserves and promotes local history; connected to courthouse and museum networks
- **Hill College** — Community college with Cleburne extension campus; workforce and community education ties
- **Visit Cleburne** — City's official tourism and visitor presence; `visitcleburne.com`
