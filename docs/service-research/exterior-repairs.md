## 1. What the Service Is

Exterior repairs encompass all work performed on the components of a home's building envelope that fall outside the roof system itself. The building envelope is the physical barrier between the conditioned interior of a home and the outside environment. Every element that wraps, covers, or seals the exterior of a structure is part of this system, and each component depends on the others to keep moisture, wind, pests, and heat out.

For homeowners, "exterior repairs" typically means any of the following:

- **Siding** — the cladding that covers the walls (vinyl, fiber cement, wood, brick veneer, stone veneer)
- **Soffit** — the underside of the roof overhang; sits below the fascia and above the exterior wall
- **Fascia** — the horizontal board running along the lower edge of the roofline; anchors the gutters
- **Trim** — decorative and functional boards framing windows, doors, corners, and other architectural details
- **Window frames and surrounds** — the framing and casing around window openings
- **Door frames and surrounds** — the framing, casing, and threshold around exterior doors
- **Eaves** — the roof edges and overhang assemblies including soffit and fascia together
- **Skirting and band boards** — horizontal trim elements separating stories or foundation from wall
- **Columns and posts** — structural or decorative vertical supports on porches and entries
- **Porches and decks** — attached outdoor platforms, their decking, railings, and structural supports
- **Fences** — perimeter fencing in wood, composite, or other materials
- **Chimneys (exterior portions)** — cap, crown, flashing, brick/mortar, and exterior chase cladding
- **Caulking and sealants** — the continuous sealing system at every joint, seam, and penetration

Together these components form a system. A failure in one area — say, deteriorated caulking around a window — can lead to water intrusion that rots the window frame, damages adjacent siding, and eventually compromises interior walls. Exterior repairs address individual component failures while also looking at how the full system is holding up.

## 2. What They Do

### Inspection and Damage Assessment

Exterior repair work begins with a systematic inspection of all envelope components. A trained technician walks the full perimeter of the home, examining siding panels, trim boards, soffit sections, fascia runs, caulk lines, and any penetrations (utility entries, hose bibs, vents). They look for:

- Cracking, splitting, or buckling in siding and trim
- Impact damage (dents, punctures, or cracks from hail or debris)
- Discoloration or staining indicating water infiltration
- Paint failure: peeling, bubbling, or chalking
- Soft or spongy wood indicating rot
- Gaps in caulking or sealant at joints and transitions
- Fastener failure: popped nails, loose panels, separated seams
- Structural deformation: bowing, sagging, or misalignment

Damage is documented with photographs and measurements to support homeowner insurance claims and to scope the repair accurately.

### Material Matching

One of the more demanding aspects of exterior repair — particularly for older homes — is sourcing materials that match the existing cladding, trim profiles, and colors. This matters for both aesthetic continuity and insurance settlements. Contractors must identify:

- The exact product line and profile of existing vinyl or fiber cement siding
- The thickness, width, and texture of wood trim
- The brick coursing pattern, color, and mortar joint style
- The paint color (for touch-ups or full repaints after replacement)

In some cases, discontinued products require sourcing close matches or replacing larger sections to achieve visual consistency.

### Repair vs. Replacement

Not every damaged exterior component requires full replacement. The contractor evaluates:

- **Spot repair**: Patching isolated sections of siding, filling cracks, re-caulking joints, or replacing a single board
- **Section replacement**: Removing and replacing a defined run of siding, a fascia board, or a section of trim where damage is concentrated
- **Full replacement**: When damage is widespread, a product line is discontinued, or the cost of matching exceeds the cost of replacing the whole elevation

The decision also factors in age, condition of surrounding materials, and whether the homeowner has an active insurance claim that covers full replacement.

### Repair and Replacement Execution

Actual repair work involves:

- Removing damaged material without disturbing adjacent sound material or the water-resistant barrier (housewrap) behind it
- Installing proper flashing and drainage planes before re-cladding
- Fastening replacement materials to manufacturer specifications (critical for warranty validity and wind resistance)
- Maintaining proper overlap, clearances, and expansion gaps (especially for vinyl and fiber cement)
- Sealing all joints and penetrations with appropriate caulk or sealant

### Painting and Finishing

Repaired sections often require painting or staining to match the surrounding surface. This may involve:

- Priming bare wood or fiber cement before topcoat application
- Color-matching to existing paint (or full repaint of an elevation for uniformity)
- Sealing exposed end-grain on wood trim boards
- Applying elastomeric coatings in areas prone to cracking

### Connection to Roof Work

Roof work and exterior repairs are frequently performed simultaneously, particularly after storm events. The reasons are practical:

- **Shared access**: Scaffolding and ladders are already in place for roof replacement
- **Integrated systems**: Fascia boards anchor gutters and receive drip edge from the roof; damage to one often affects the other. Replacing a roof without repairing deteriorated fascia produces improper drip edge installation.
- **Insurance bundling**: Storm damage to the roof commonly extends to siding (hail), soffit (wind), and fascia. Filing a single bundled claim is more efficient and typically more fully compensated than separate claims.
- **Moisture pathways**: Water that enters through a compromised roof may have already damaged soffit, fascia, and wall framing below. Replacing the roof without addressing that interior moisture damage leads to ongoing problems.
- **Building code requirements**: Some jurisdictions require inspections that cover both roof and adjacent exterior components when work permits are pulled.

For these reasons, a roofing contractor who also performs exterior repairs can offer homeowners a more complete scope of work after a storm — one point of contact, one insurance claim, one project timeline.

## 3. What They Specialize In (DFW / Crowley Area Specifics)

### Storm Damage

North Texas is one of the most active hailstorm regions in the United States. DFW sits within "Hail Alley" — the corridor where Gulf moisture from the south collides with cold air from the Rocky Mountains, producing large hailstones and severe convective storms from March through June. Crowley and the southwest DFW suburbs are regularly impacted.

**Hail damage to siding:**
- *Vinyl siding*: Hail impact cracks or punches through vinyl panels, particularly in temperatures below 50°F when vinyl becomes brittle. Common damage includes spider cracks at impact points, broken locking seams, and punctures. Cracked vinyl panels allow water infiltration behind the wall assembly.
- *Fiber cement (Hardie board)*: Fiber cement is harder than vinyl but chips and fractures under large hail impact. Damage appears as circular chips or divots in the panel face, often exposing the substrate. Because fiber cement is a cementitious product, exposed edges and chips absorb moisture rapidly if not repaired.
- *Wood siding*: Wood dents and splits under hail impact. The damage may be subtle initially but opens the grain to moisture and accelerating rot.
- *Brick*: Brick itself typically survives hail, but mortar joints can be damaged by large stones, and soft brick types used in older DFW construction can show surface spalling.

**Wind damage:**
- High winds from DFW's frequent severe thunderstorms and occasional tornado events lift and peel siding panels, particularly at corners and the tops of walls where wind uplift is greatest
- Wind-blown debris (tree branches, gravel, debris from neighboring structures) causes puncture and impact damage across siding, window trim, door frames, and fencing
- Sustained wind pressure can loosen or back out fasteners over time, causing panels to separate and rattle

### Texas Heat and UV Damage

DFW experiences 100+ consecutive days at or above 90°F in a typical year, with frequent stretches above 100°F. Combined with intense solar radiation, this creates specific deterioration patterns:

- *Vinyl siding*: High UV exposure causes color fading and chalking. More significantly, extreme heat causes vinyl to expand and contract dramatically across seasons. Improperly installed vinyl (panels fastened too tightly) will buckle and warp. In older installations, panels that have lost flexibility from UV degradation may crack rather than flex.
- *Paint on wood and fiber cement*: UV radiation breaks down paint binders, causing chalking, fading, and eventually film failure. South- and west-facing elevations (the most sun-exposed in DFW) degrade faster. Paint failure on fiber cement or wood exposes the substrate to moisture.
- *Wood trim and structural members*: Prolonged heat and UV exposure dries wood, causing it to crack, check (surface fissures along the grain), and lose dimensional stability. Dried wood also becomes more susceptible to moisture absorption when rain does arrive.

### Moisture Damage and Wood Rot

Texas humidity and rain cycles create moisture risks distinct from arid climates:

- DFW's humidity — often 70–90% on summer mornings even during dry spells — keeps exterior wood perpetually in a moisture-cycling environment
- Spring and fall bring significant rainfall; North Texas averages roughly 36–40 inches of precipitation annually
- The combination of heat-driven drying and periodic saturation is hard on wood components

**Common moisture damage locations in DFW homes:**

- *Soffit boards*: The soffit sits at the lowest, most sheltered part of the roof overhang and is the first place to show wood rot when gutter overflow, ice dam melt (rare but occurs), or driven rain reaches the wood substrate. Vinyl soffit covering deteriorated wood framing is common.
- *Fascia boards*: Water running behind clogged or improperly hung gutters saturates fascia boards chronically. Fascia rot is one of the most common exterior repair jobs on DFW homes.
- *Window frames and sills*: Failed caulking at window perimeters allows water to penetrate and sit in the horizontal sill, where it rots the framing. This is aggravated by the DFW wet season when windows may be exposed to multiple rain events per week.
- *Door frames and thresholds*: Entry door thresholds and sill pans accumulate water from driving rain. Wood door frames without proper flashing develop rot at the base.
- *Trim boards at grade level*: Trim boards close to the foundation may wick moisture from soil contact or splash-back from hard surfaces.

### Expansive Clay Soil and Foundation Movement

The Blackland Prairie clay soils prevalent across Crowley and the DFW Metroplex expand significantly when wet and shrink when dry. This seasonal soil movement causes foundation movement in slab-on-grade homes — the dominant construction type in the area.

Foundation movement creates exterior symptoms including:

- Cracking in exterior stucco or EIFS cladding at corners and above/below windows
- Separation of caulk joints at corners and window/door perimeters
- Stepped cracking in brick veneer along mortar joints — a classic indicator of differential foundation movement
- Gaps opening between trim boards and adjacent siding or masonry as framing shifts
- Door and window frames racking slightly out of square, stressing the surrounding casing and trim

Exterior repairs addressing these symptoms include re-caulking, tuckpointing brick, patching stucco, and replacing deformed trim. However, if foundation movement is active and significant, surface repairs are temporary. Homeowners should be advised to have a foundation specialist evaluate active or progressive cracking before investing in cosmetic exterior repairs.

### Common Siding Types in DFW Homes

Understanding the local housing stock is important for repair work. DFW homes built across different decades use different cladding systems:

- **Brick veneer**: The dominant exterior on homes built from the 1970s through the 2000s in the DFW suburbs. Most suburban Crowley homes are primarily brick. Brick itself requires little maintenance but mortar joints deteriorate over 20–30 years. Partial brick elevations — back and side walls clad in vinyl or fiber cement — are common.
- **Vinyl siding**: Widely used on the non-brick elevations of many DFW homes, and the primary cladding on homes built from the late 1990s through the 2010s in more affordable price points. Also found on additions and re-cladded older homes. Vinyl is inexpensive to repair but can be difficult to color-match as products age and fade.
- **Fiber cement / Hardie board**: Increasingly common on homes built from the 2000s onward, and a popular choice for re-cladding projects. James Hardie products dominate the DFW fiber cement market. Requires painting; holds paint well under DFW conditions compared to wood; resistant to moisture and insects but vulnerable to hail impact.
- **Wood siding**: Found on older homes (pre-1970s) and on higher-end custom construction. Requires the most maintenance of any siding type in a DFW climate. Common profiles include clapboard (horizontal lap), board-and-batten, and cedar shakes.
- **Stone veneer**: Used for accent elevations and entryways on mid- to upper-range DFW construction from the 2000s onward. Both natural thin-cut stone and manufactured stone veneer products are in use. Mortar joints between stones deteriorate similarly to brick.
- **Stucco / EIFS**: Found on some custom homes and Mediterranean-influenced construction in DFW. Stucco is vulnerable to cracking from foundation movement; EIFS (exterior insulation and finish system, or synthetic stucco) has a history of moisture problems when not properly detailed.

### Insurance Claims for Storm-Damaged Exteriors

In Texas, homeowner's insurance policies typically cover sudden and accidental storm damage from hail and wind. Key considerations:

- **Bundled claims**: When a hailstorm damages the roof, it almost always damaged the siding, soffit, fascia, window screens, gutters, and other exterior components simultaneously. Filing a single comprehensive claim covering all affected components typically results in better compensation than sequential or separate claims.
- **Cosmetic damage provisions**: Some Texas insurance policies issued after 2011 include endorsements that exclude coverage for "cosmetic" exterior damage — meaning visible dents or marks that do not compromise the function of the material. Homeowners should review their policy before assuming full coverage.
- **Matching disputes**: Insurers sometimes resist paying for full siding replacements when only a portion of panels are damaged, arguing that matching panels can be found. In practice, matching aged and faded vinyl siding is difficult. Texas law (Insurance Code Section 707.007 and related regulations) addresses matching in some contexts; this is an area where experienced contractors can advocate effectively for homeowners.
- **Contractor-assisted claims**: A roofing and exterior contractor experienced with insurance claims can document exterior damage comprehensively at the time of roof inspection, increasing the likelihood that all legitimate damage is captured in the adjuster's scope.
- **Deductibles and timelines**: Texas hail deductibles are commonly 1–2% of dwelling value (not a flat dollar amount). Homeowners should understand their deductible structure before approving work.

## 4. Subservices List

### Siding

- **Vinyl siding repair** — Replacing cracked, punctured, or impact-damaged vinyl panels; repairing failed seams and locking channels
- **Vinyl siding replacement** — Full elevation or whole-home re-cladding in vinyl, including removal of existing material and installation of new housewrap
- **Fiber cement (Hardie board) siding repair** — Patching or replacing chipped, cracked, or hail-damaged fiber cement panels; re-priming and painting exposed areas
- **Fiber cement siding replacement** — Full section or whole-home replacement with James Hardie or equivalent products; includes priming and painting
- **Wood siding repair** — Patching splits, replacing individual boards, treating or replacing rot-damaged sections
- **Wood siding replacement** — Re-cladding in new wood products; includes sealing and painting/staining
- **Hail damage siding repair** — Assessment and repair/replacement of siding damaged by hail impact; documentation for insurance claims
- **Wind damage exterior repair** — Reattaching or replacing siding, trim, and other components lifted or damaged by high winds

### Soffit and Fascia

- **Soffit repair** — Replacing damaged sections of soffit board or soffit panel (wood or vinyl); restoring ventilation openings
- **Soffit replacement** — Full soffit replacement in vinyl, aluminum, or wood; includes removal of failed existing material and installation of continuous vented soffit where applicable
- **Fascia board repair** — Replacing individual rotted, cracked, or damaged fascia boards; re-sealing and painting
- **Fascia board replacement** — Full fascia replacement along roofline; often coordinated with gutter removal and reinstallation and roof drip edge work
- **Eave repair** — Combined soffit and fascia repair as an integrated eave assembly; addressing moisture damage that has spread across the full eave system

### Trim

- **Window trim repair** — Replacing cracked, split, rotted, or impact-damaged trim boards around windows; re-caulking window perimeters
- **Door frame and surround repair** — Replacing damaged or rotted door casing, jamb extensions, or threshold components; resealing and painting
- **Corner board repair and replacement** — Replacing damaged corner trim boards that terminate siding panels at building corners
- **Band board / belly board repair** — Replacing the horizontal trim elements between stories or separating foundation from wall cladding
- **Exterior wood rot repair** — Locating and removing all rot-affected wood in trim, framing, and structural members; treating adjacent wood; installing replacement material

### Masonry

- **Brick mortar repair (tuckpointing)** — Removing deteriorated mortar from brick joints and re-pointing with new mortar to restore weather resistance and structural integrity
- **Brick repair** — Replacing cracked, spalled, or damaged bricks; matching brick color and coursing pattern
- **Stone veneer repair** — Re-setting loose or fallen stone veneer pieces; repointing mortar joints; replacing damaged stones
- **Stucco repair** — Patching cracks and impact damage in traditional three-coat stucco; re-texturing and painting
- **EIFS repair** — Patching synthetic stucco (EIFS) systems; addressing moisture infiltration at joints and penetrations

### Structural Exterior Elements

- **Column and post repair** — Repairing or replacing rotted, damaged, or structurally compromised porch columns and posts; both structural (load-bearing) and decorative
- **Porch decking repair** — Replacing rotted, broken, or damaged decking boards on covered porches
- **Deck repair** — Repairing structural framing, decking boards, railings, and posts on attached decks; addressing rot, fastener failure, and storm damage
- **Fence repair** — Repairing or replacing damaged wood fence boards, posts, rails, and gates; common after wind events that knock over panels or snap posts

### Chimney Exterior

- **Chimney cap repair and replacement** — Metal chimney caps that prevent water and animal entry deteriorate from rust and storm damage; replacement with new galvanized or stainless steel caps
- **Chimney crown repair** — The concrete crown at the top of a masonry chimney prevents water from entering behind the flue liner; repairing or resurfacing cracked crowns with elastomeric sealant or mortar
- **Chimney flashing repair** — Repairing or replacing the metal flashing where the chimney penetrates the roof plane; a common leak source
- **Chimney brick and mortar repair** — Tuckpointing and brick replacement on the visible exterior of the chimney stack
- **Chimney chase cover repair** — Metal chase covers on prefabricated (factory-built) chimney chases rust and fail, allowing water into the chase; replacement with stainless steel covers
- **Chimney siding/cladding repair** — On prefabricated chimney chases clad in siding, repairing storm damage or deteriorated cladding on the chase exterior

### Sealing and Flashing

- **Exterior caulking and sealing** — Re-caulking all exterior joints: window and door perimeters, corner trim seams, penetrations, siding terminations, and transitions between dissimilar materials. The caulk line is the last line of defense against water at every joint.
- **Window and door flashing repair** — Installing or replacing pan flashing, head flashing, and sill flashing at window and door openings; critical for preventing water infiltration behind the wall assembly
- **Penetration sealing** — Sealing around utility penetrations, dryer vents, hose bibs, electrical boxes, and HVAC lines that pass through the exterior wall
- **Transition flashing** — Installing or repairing metal flashing at material transitions (e.g., where siding meets a roof deck, where a second-story wall meets a first-story roof)

### Paint and Coating

- **Exterior painting** — Repainting repaired sections, full elevations, or whole-home exteriors after repairs; includes surface preparation, priming, and two-coat topcoat application
- **Wood staining and sealing** — Staining and sealing wood siding, trim, decking, and fencing after repair or replacement
- **Elastomeric coating application** — Applying flexible waterproof coatings to masonry, stucco, or concrete surfaces prone to cracking; bridges hairline cracks and provides waterproofing

## Notes for Website Copy

- Lead with the storm connection: DFW homeowners most often need exterior repairs after hail or wind events. The bundled claim angle is a strong differentiator.
- Emphasize the inspection-first approach: homeowners often don't know what exterior damage they have until it's assessed by a professional.
- Wood rot is an evergreen concern in DFW — fascia and soffit rot is common across all ages of homes due to gutter overflow and humidity cycles. This is a strong local pain point.
- Brick is the most common exterior on DFW homes, but tuckpointing and mortar repair are underappreciated services. Position these as preventive before water infiltration causes larger damage.
- Insurance navigation: many homeowners don't know their siding and exterior trim may be covered under the same claim as their roof. This is a key education point.
- Foundation movement cracking is a distinct DFW issue — it creates recurring exterior repair needs and homeowners benefit from understanding why their caulk keeps cracking.
